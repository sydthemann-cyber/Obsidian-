###### Abstract
Crustose coralline algae play a crucial role in the building of reefs in the photic zones of nearshore ecosystems globally, and are highly susceptible to ocean acidification1–3. Nevertheless, the extent to which ecologically important crustose coralline algae can gain tolerance to ocean acidification over multiple generations of exposure is unknown. We show that, while calcification of juvenile crustose coralline algae is initially highly sensitive to ocean acidification, after six generations of exposure the effects of ocean acidification disappear. A reciprocal transplant experiment conducted on the seventh generation, where half of all replicates were interchanged across treatments, confirmed that they had acquired tolerance to low pH and not simply to laboratory conditions. Neither exposure to greater pH variability, nor chemical conditions within the micro-scale calcifying fluid internally, appeared to play a role in fostering this capacity. Our results demonstrate that reef-accreting taxa can gain tolerance to ocean acidification over multiple generations of exposure, suggesting that some of these cosmopolitan species could maintain their critical ecological role in reef formation.

### Summary

- Tested whether corals and calcifying algae can **acclimatize to ocean acidification (OA)** over one year.
- No evidence of physiological acclimatization was observed across any species.
- OA reduced **calcification** in several species by altering the chemistry of the **calcifying fluid (CF)**.
- Species differed in their ability to regulate **pHCF**, **DICCF**, and **ΩCF**, explaining variation in OA tolerance.
- Massive **Porites** exhibited the greatest resistance by maintaining relatively stable calcifying fluid chemistry.
- Long-term OA remains a significant threat to coral reef growth and reef accretion. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### Detailed Notes

#### Background

##### Ocean Acidification (OA)

Cause

- ↑ atmospheric CO₂
- ↓ seawater pH
- altered carbonate chemistry

Consequences

- ↓ calcification
- ↑ CaCO₃ dissolution
- reduced reef growth

---

##### Calcifying Fluid (CF)

Site where corals precipitate CaCO₃.

Corals regulate

- **pHCF**
- **DICCF** (dissolved inorganic carbon)
- **ΩCF** (aragonite saturation state)

Maintaining elevated CF chemistry promotes calcification despite surrounding seawater conditions.

---

#### Study Design

##### Objective

Determine whether long-term exposure to elevated CO₂ allows

- physiological acclimatization
- restoration of calcifying fluid chemistry

---

##### Experimental Design

Duration

- 1 year
- (*Halimeda* = 2 months)

Conditions

- outdoor flow-through flumes
- natural light
- natural seasonal temperatures
- four pCO₂ treatments

Species

Corals

- *Pocillopora verrucosa*
- *Psammocora profundacella*
- *Acropora pulchra*
- massive *Porites*

Calcifying algae

- *Lithophyllum kotschyanum*
- *Halimeda minima*

---

#### Calcification Responses

##### Species-Specific Responses

**Porites**

- calcification largely unchanged
- most resistant species

**Pocillopora**

- little change in calcification

**Acropora**

- calcification decreased with increasing pCO₂

**Psammocora**

- calcification decreased with increasing pCO₂

**Lithophyllum**

- calcification decreased with increasing pCO₂

Demonstrates strong interspecific variation in OA sensitivity.

---

#### Calcifying Fluid Chemistry

##### pHCF

Increasing pCO₂

↓

decreased **pHCF** in

- *Acropora*
- *Psammocora*
- *Pocillopora*
- both algal species

**Porites**

- maintained relatively constant **pHCF**

---

##### DICCF

Increasing pCO₂

↓

↑ **DICCF**

Observed in

- *Acropora*
- *Psammocora*
- *Pocillopora*

Represents partial physiological compensation for declining pH.

---

##### ΩCF

Determined by

- pHCF
- DICCF
- Ca²⁺ concentration

Observed responses

- relatively stable in most corals
- decreased in *Psammocora*

Lower ΩCF

↓

reduced calcification potential

---

#### Acclimatization

##### Hypothesis

Long-term exposure

↓

physiological adjustment

↓

recovery of CF chemistry

---

##### Results

No recovery observed

- CF chemistry remained altered after one year
- OA effects persisted throughout experiment

Conclusion

- acclimatization did not occur within experimental timeframe

---

#### Seasonal Variation

##### Environmental Drivers

Measured

- temperature
- irradiance

Seasonal variation affected

- DICCF
- ΩCF

However

- did not eliminate OA effects
- did not restore calcifying fluid chemistry

---

#### Mechanisms of OA Resistance

##### Resistant Species

Example

**Porites**

Characteristics

- maintains elevated pHCF
- maintains calcification
- greater physiological regulation

---

##### Sensitive Species

Examples

- *Acropora*
- *Psammocora*
- *Lithophyllum*

Characteristics

- reduced pHCF
- reduced calcification
- weaker regulation of calcifying fluid

---

#### Ecological Implications

##### Coral Reefs

Reduced calcification

↓

slower reef accretion

↓

reduced structural complexity

↓

declining habitat availability

---

##### Community Composition

Species-specific tolerance

↓

competitive shifts

↓

community restructuring

↓

more OA-tolerant taxa dominate

---

#### Conclusions

- corals exhibit species-specific physiological responses to OA
- maintaining calcifying fluid chemistry is central to OA resistance
- one year of exposure produced **no evidence of acclimatization**
- physiological regulation, rather than acclimatization, determines resilience to future ocean acidification
