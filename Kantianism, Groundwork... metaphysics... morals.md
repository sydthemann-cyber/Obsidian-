**Preface:**
- groundwork: foundation of 
- metaphysics: a prior study of determinate objects 
	- empirical: based on past experience (not a priori)
	- logical: a prior study of something that is 'merely formal'
- Kant isn’t asking whether anyone ever _has_ acted morally, or how to tell. He is analyzing **what morality must be**, given our concept of duty.
- He rules out **everything empirical**: anthropology, what cultures believe, what we admire.
- Practical point: an empirically mixed moral theory is not only theoretically impure but corrupts conduct. If morality rests on incentives, it gives way when the incentives change.

**Section 1:** **From common moral knowledge to the philosophical principle**
- the good will 
	- “It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world, or indeed even beyond it, that could be considered good without limitation [Ellington: good without qualification] except a good will.”
	- other characteristics are only good conditionally, or for the ends they achieves 
- The good will is good in itself, not because of what it achieves. Even if by bad luck it accomplishes nothing, “like a jewel, it would still shine by itself, as something that has its full worth in itself.
- teleological arguentL
	- if nature had meant for reason to be about achieving happiness (ie happiness is good in and of itself) then why not make happiness a matter of instinct, because reason often makes us unhappy, and the more of it we have the less happy we are --> its function must be to produce a will good in itself 
- duty: moral worth versus moral acceptability 
	- contrary to duty: no moral worth
	- in accord with duty, selfish purpose: no moral worth
		- shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge
	- in accord with duty, from inclination: no true moral worth 
		- sympathetic philanthropist
	- in accord with duty, from duty: true moral worth 
		- eg the grieving philanthropist 
- **3 propositions** 
	1) an action has moral worth only if it is done from duty (and accords with duty), not from inclination.
	2) “an action from duty has its moral worth not in the purpose to be attained by it but in the maxim in accordance with which it is decided upon.”
	3) “**duty is the necessity of an action from respect for law**.”
		- maxim: “The **subjective** principle of volition,” meaning the general policy _you_ are acting on.
- **the pure form of law
	- Strip away every material incentive (every goal). What remains is the bare form of law: universality, applying to everyone. --> 
	- 1) “**I should never act except in such a way that I can also will that my maxim should become a universal law.**”

**Section II: from popular moral philosophy to the metaphysics of morals**
- why not use a priori 
	- you never know someones maxim 
- **imperatives**
	- Everything in nature acts according to laws. **Only a rational being acts according to its _representation_ of laws**, which is to say it has a **will**, or practical reason.
	- For imperfect wills like ours, the law appears as a **command**, an “ought”: an **imperative**.
		- hypothetical imperatives: skills, prudence, analytic 
	- if the action would be good merely **as a means to something else** the imperative is **hypothetical**; if the action is represented as **in itself good**… then it is **categorical**.”

- **first formulation: formula of universal law**
	- Because a categorical imperative has no content from a desired end, it can command only that your maxim conform to universal law as such.
	- “Act only according to that maxim whereby you **can** **at the same time will** that it should become a **universal law**.”
		- can the maxim be universalized **without contradiction** 
	- contradiction in conception: CAN it be willed without contradition 
		- no = violation of perfect duty
	- contradiction in the will: can you CONSISTENTLY will it 
		- no = violation of imperfect duty

**second formulation: formula of humanity**
“**Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of another, always at the same time as an end and never simply as a means.**”
- a categorical imperative needs an end that is not a subjective end (an object of desire, which has only relative worth) but something whose existence in itself has absolute worth: an end in itself.
	- Objects of inclination have only conditional worth.
	- **Rational beings are “persons”** and are “**ends in themselves**.” Non-rational things are “things,” with relative value only.
	- - “**Rational nature exists as an end in itself.**” Each person necessarily represents their own existence this way, and on the same rational ground so must everyone.
**The four examples under FH:**
- **False promise:** you use the lender **merely as a means**. They “cannot possibly agree” to your way of treating them and can’t “contain the end of this action in himself.” Violates a perfect duty to others. The same reasoning covers attacks on the freedom and property of others.
- **Suicide:** to escape a burdensome condition, you use your own **person** merely as a means to keep yourself comfortable “up to the end of life.” You “**dispose of** a human being” (yourself) as a thing. Perfect duty to self. Kant leaves aside cases like amputation or risking your life to save it.
- **Talents:** not developing your humanity merely _fails to harmonize_ with humanity as an end. It doesn’t actively degrade it. Imperfect duty to self.
- **Beneficence:** merely not interfering with others is only **negative** agreement with humanity as an end. “The ends of a subject who is an end in itself must as far as possible be **also my ends**.” Imperfect duty to others.

**Autonomy**
- **Why rational beings have this status:** they are capable of **autonomy** (auto + nomos, “self-law”): “the property of the will by which it is a **law to itself**.” “The idea of the will of every rational being as a will **giving universal law**.”
- **Heteronomy:** the will’s law comes from outside, from inclinations, objects of desire, God’s command, or social custom.
    - **All previous moral theories failed** because they based obligation on some _interest_ (happiness, perfection, divine will). That can only yield hypothetical imperatives.
    - **Link:** Mill is heteronomous on this view, and so are DCT and relativism.
- **The will “is subject to the law” only because it is also its author.** That answers “why be moral?”: the demand isn’t external, it is _your own_ reason. Asking for an external incentive misunderstands morality, and acting for such an incentive would remove moral worth anyway.
- **Only acts done from duty are autonomous, and therefore free.** Acting on inclination is following the laws of nature and psychology.

**kingdom of Ends**
- > “A systematic union of rational beings through common objective laws… **a kingdom of ends** (admittedly only an ideal).”
- Each member is both **sovereign** (legislator) and **subject**. Because reason is the same in everyone, all legislate the **same** laws. Compare an ordinary kingdom: one sovereign, many subjects.
- > “In the kingdom of ends everything has either a **price** or a **dignity**. What has a price can be replaced by something else as its **equivalent**; what… is raised above all price and therefore **admits of no equivalent** has a dignity.”

harmonization 
- "Humanity might indeed subsist if no one contributed to the happiness of others but yet did not intentionally withdraw anything from it; but there is still **only a negative and not a positive agreement with humanity as an end in itself** unless everyone also tries, as far as he can, to **further the ends of others**. For, **the ends of a subject who is an end in itself must as far as possible be also my ends**, if that representation is to have its full effect in me."
- "“It is **not enough** that the action does not **conflict** with humanity in our person as an end in itself; **it must also harmonize with it**… to neglect these [predispositions to greater perfection] might admittedly be consistent with the **preservation** of humanity as an end in itself but not with the **furtherance** of this end.”"
