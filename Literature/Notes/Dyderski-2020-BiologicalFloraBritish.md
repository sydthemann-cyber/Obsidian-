###### Abstract
This account presents information on all aspects of the biology of Quercus rubra L. (Red Oak, Northern Red Oak; syn. Q. borealis, Q. maxima) that are relevant to understanding its ecological characteristics and behaviour. The main topics are presented within the standard framework of the Biological Flora of the British Isles: distribution; habitat; communities; responses to biotic factors; responses to environment; structure and physiology; phenology; floral and seed characters; herbivores and disease; history; and conservation. Quercus rubra is a large deciduous tree native to eastern North America. In its native range this species is one of the most important mid-successional tree species. Since the 18th century Q. rubra has been introduced into forest management in Europe and elsewhere. Currently, Q. rubra is a naturalized species in 23 countries. It is an important source of wood, and this was one of the most important reasons for its introduction in forests. In forest management this species is valued for its high growth rate and drought resistance. Red Oak is also widely cultivated in Europe for its ornamental value—red leaves in autumn and smooth, grey bark. Red Oak is a threat to native species within its introduced range as it can alter environmental conditions beneath its canopy, especially by decreasing light availability and litter decomposition rate. Such conditions also contribute to biotic homogenization of the understorey vegetation. Q. rubra is widely dispersed by small mammals and birds, up to 1,500 m from seed sources, thereby increasing its potential invasiveness. In Europe Q. rubra seems to be less prone to herbivores and fungi, in contrast to its native range, where it is grazed by ungulates and insects, and infected by Phytophthora and oak wilt. Due to its higher tolerance to drought than native European oaks, Q. rubra is claimed to be one of the species replacing native trees which are decreasing due to climate warming. However, consideration of all possible consequences is needed in wide-scale introduction of alien species, such as Q. rubra, due to negative impacts on native ecosystems.

## geographical and altitudinal distribution 
NATIVE:
- Eastern North America: continuous from Alabama/Georgia/North Carolina (32°N) north to Minnesota/Nova Scotia (47°N)
- Elevation: sea level to 1,680 m a.s.l.
- Mean annual temps across range: 4°C (north) to 16°C (south)
- Precipitation: ~760 mm (northwest) to ~2,030 mm (southern Appalachians)
- Frost-free period: ~100 days (north) to ~220 days (south)
INTRODUCED:
- First introduced 17th century (possibly as early as 1637 description based on trees already growing in the Netherlands)
- Naturalized in 23 countries; noted in 34 of 49 European regions (21 countries)
- Spans Portugal/Ireland (west) to Ukraine/European Russia (east); Spain/Greece (south) to Norway/Sweden (north)
- Altitudinal range: 0–800 m a.s.l.
- Major forestry plantings: Ukraine (193,000 ha), France (52,000 ha), Germany (44,500 ha); total ~350,000 ha in Europe
- Also naturalized: western USA (CO, ID, OR, WA), Armenia, New Zealand
- Introduced (not yet naturalized): Argentina, Brazil, Chile, China, India, Japan, Kazakhstan, South Korea
## habitat <!--c:wdqu5-->preferences
SOIL
- Wide tolerance: pH 4.3–7.3, from sandy to silty clay loam
- Prefers deep, loose, acid, limestone-free soils of moderate fertility
- Reduced growth on very dry, waterlogged, or calcareous soils
- No natural regeneration on high-calcium soils
- Prefers gentle/mid slopes with N or E aspects
- Grows faster on clay than sandy soil (146–150% height advantage at 5 years, dropping to 37–48% by 45 years)
- Will colonize marginal sites: nutrient-poor colliery waste tips (spontaneous regeneration to tree layer within 60 years)
LIGHT
- Classed as intermediate shade-tolerant / semi-shade species
- Avoids full light AND deep shade (<10% diffuse radiation)
- Canopy light transmission in monocultures: 3.5% (35-yr), 1.4% (45-yr), 3.7% (60–105-yr stands)
CLIMATE 
- Wind- and frost-resistant; moderate drought tolerance
- Survives extreme cold (down to −40°C)
- More drought-tolerant than native European oaks — key competitive advantage in introduced range
COMMUNITY 
- Native range: dominant/pure stands in Northern Red Oak forest type; also codominant with white pine/red maple, or white oak/black oak
- Introduced range: rarely forms monospecific stands; found in coniferous plantations (Germany), oak stands (France, Spain), post-industrial/disturbed habitats
- Understorey beneath monocultures is species-poor (comparable to dense beech canopy)

# general life history / life cycle
GROWTH 
- Fast growth first 20 years; increment slows after 40 years
- Height examples: 6 m at 10 yrs (Romania); 21 m at 35 yrs (Bulgaria); 30–35 m at 80–100 yrs (Romania/France)
- UK examples: 20–22 m at 40 yrs; 24.2 m at 72 yrs
- Mature height typically 25–35 m (tallest recorded 43 m, France)
- Diameter growth peaks at 20–60 years old (~0.5 cm/year average)
- Lifespan: claimed up to 400–500 years under optimum conditions; oldest verified by tree-ring count = 322 years (Massachusetts)
REPRODUCTION
- **Age to first seed:** 20–25 years typically; as early as 10 years under favorable conditions
- **Acorn development takes 3 growing seasons total:**
    - Year 1: flower buds initiated
    - Year 2: anthesis and pollination occur
    - Acorns mature at end of following growing season (up to 18 months after pollination)
- Wind-pollinated, monoecious, self-incompatible (gametophytic control)
- Peak seed productivity: 40–50 years old
- Mast years occur every 2–5 years
ACORN 
- Seed mass: 1.8–6.1 g (native), 2.4–6.0 g (introduced)
- Recalcitrant seeds — lose viability if desiccated below 20% moisture
- Require cold stratification (5°C through winter) to break dormancy
- Dispersed by scatter-hoarders: blue jays (1–5 km, native range); European jay, wood mice, yellow-necked mice (Europe); mice move acorns only ~2 m, jays are long-distance vectors despite generally preferring native oak acorns over Q. rubra
GERMINATION / SEEDLING 
- Hypogeal germination
- Germinates spring following autumn seed fall
- By July of germination year: up to 24 cm height, 3 mm diameter
- Seedling survival after 1 year: 8.4–17.8% (mean 12.5%)
- Light availability critical for survival but less limiting to biomass allocation than expected
- "Oskar syndrome": seedlings can persist for years at near-zero height growth in deep shade, then rapidly surge (increased RGR, leaf area, photosynthetic capacity) when canopy gaps open — key adaptive trait
SPROUTING 
- Sprouts vigorously from stump/root collar after damage (cutting, fire, browsing)
- Sprouting ability declines with tree size — only 30% of stumps >43 cm diameter can sprout
- > 95% of post-harvest regrowth in native range is from sprouts
    
- New sprouts grow ~61 cm/year for ~30 years
PHENOLOGY 
- Deciduous — sheds all leaves before winter (unlike European oaks, which retain marcescent leaves)
- Bud swelling: April; leaves unfold late April–May
- Flowering: April–May (during leaf development)
- Acorn shed: August–October (Europe: September–October)
