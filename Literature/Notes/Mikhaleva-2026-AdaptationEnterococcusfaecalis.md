###### Abstract

### Summary

This paper uses a **human colonic organoid model** to investigate how *Enterococcus faecalis* adapts to the intestinal mucus layer during early colonization. Unlike traditional abiotic biofilm models, *E. faecalis* forms **biofilm-like microcolonies embedded within mucus**, undergoes extensive **metabolic reprogramming**, and relies on **host-derived glycans**, stress-response systems, and specific membrane glycolipids for successful colonization. Using live imaging combined with **Tn-seq**, the authors identify genes involved in carbohydrate transport, amino acid biosynthesis, two-component signaling, and glycolipid synthesis that are specifically required for growth in mucus. Importantly, many classical in vitro biofilm genes were **dispensable**, suggesting that mucosal colonization represents a distinct physiological state. The study establishes human colonoids as a powerful platform for studying host-associated biofilms and early commensal adaptation. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

### Detailed Notes

#### Background

- *Enterococcus faecalis* (Ef)
  - Gram-positive facultative anaerobe
  - early gut colonizer
  - common intestinal commensal
  - opportunistic pathogen during dysbiosis
- mucus acts as both
  - physical barrier
  - nutrient reservoir
- mechanisms governing adaptation to native intestinal mucus remain poorly understood
- conventional biofilm assays poorly mimic the mucosal environment

---

#### Human colonoid model

Platform

- human colonic organoids grown as polarized monolayers
- apical surface exposed
- differentiated at air-liquid interface
- goblet cells produce native MUC2 mucus
- ~10 μm mucus layer generated

Advantages

- direct access to mucus
- preserves native mucus architecture
- compatible with
  - live imaging
  - confocal microscopy
  - Tn-seq
  - microfluidics

---

#### Ef colonization of mucus

Within 6 h

- ~50-fold increase in CFU
- doubling time ≈ 1 h
- slower than BHI
- indicates nutrient limitation

Spatial localization

- preferentially colonizes mucus-rich regions
- remains separated from epithelial cells
- forms biofilm-like microcolonies

Average colony volume

- ~28 μm³
- consistent with ~50-fold expansion

---

#### Biofilm architecture

Under physiological flow

Ef exhibits two primary growth modes

Low-density communities

- between mucus fibers
- dispersed

Dense microcolonies

- embedded within mucus
- confined inside mucus-lined cavities

Growing colonies

- mechanically displace mucus
- expand cavities within mucus matrix
- remain surrounded by mucus

Overall

- mucus acts as structural scaffold
- mucus constrains colony geometry
- resembles host-associated biofilms rather than abiotic biofilms

#### Biofilm genes

Unexpected observation

Many classical biofilm regulators were **not required** during early mucus colonization.

Examples

- Fsr quorum sensing
- GelE
- SprE
- sortase adhesins

Suggests

host-associated biofilms differ substantially from abiotic biofilms.

#### BgsB

Most important biofilm-associated determinant identified.

Gene

- **bgsB**
- glycosyltransferase

Function

- glycolipid synthesis
- diglucosyldiacylglycerol production
- membrane composition

Phenotype

Loss of **bgsB**

↓

- severe fitness defect
- failure to form microcolonies
- inability to expand within mucus

Unlike many classical biofilm genes,

BgsB is specifically required during early mucosal colonization.

#### Mucus as a physical environment

Mucus is not simply nutrient source.

Acts as

- structural scaffold
- mechanical constraint
- protective matrix
- habitat

Biofilm development therefore depends on

- metabolism
- mechanics
- host-derived architecture

#### Model proposed

Planktonic Ef

↓

contact with mucus

↓

stress sensing

↓

metabolic reprogramming

↓

host glycan utilization

↓

localized growth

↓

microcolony formation

↓

stable mucus colonization

---

#### Significance

Demonstrates that

- native mucus induces a physiology distinct from laboratory biofilms
- metabolism and environmental sensing dominate early adaptation
- host-derived physical structure shapes biofilm architecture
- organoids provide a high-resolution model bridging in vitro and in vivo studies

---

#### Limitations

- single bacterial species
- aerobic conditions
- short colonization period
- mucus layer thinner than native colon
- lacks complex microbiota
- lacks immune system
