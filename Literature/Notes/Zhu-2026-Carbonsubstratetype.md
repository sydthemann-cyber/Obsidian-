###### Abstract
Spatial organization is a defining feature of multispecies biofilms and critically influences microbial interactions and emergent community properties. However, understanding and manipulating how microbes assemble into spatially structured biofilms remains challenging because most experimental frameworks emphasize species composition and pairwise interactions, while often overlooking the spatial constraints on biofilms imposed by the environment. In this study, we focus on how carbon substrate type, distinguishing between diffusible sugars and polymeric substrates, affects biofilm self-organization in a four-member synthetic bacterial community (SynCom). Across all tested conditions, the SynCom consistently formed more biofilm biomass than any of its subsets, indicating a robust synergistic phenotype. Using chemically defined, 3D-printed hydrogel substrates with consistent physical properties, we varied carbon source composition to identify its impact on biofilm assembly. Microscopic imaging showed that carbon substrate type strongly influenced biofilm self-organization with diffusible simple carbon substrates yielding relatively intermixed communities, whereas polymer-rich carbon substrates promoted a highly structured biofilm organization characterized by the dominance and peripheral localization of polymer-degrading species. Bioinformatic analyses of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) annotation and genome-scale metabolic modeling suggested that metabolite exchange networks in the SynCom may drive more complex metabolic interactions beyond the commonly observed degrader-exploiter-scavenger relationship within planktonic microbial communities. Together, our findings demonstrate carbon substrate type as an important ecological determinant of biofilm self-organization, highlighting the need to integrate environmental factors alongside species composition and metabolic potential to fully understand and manipulate natural and engineered multispecies biofilms.

### Summary

This paper demonstrates that **carbon substrate type is a major ecological determinant of multispecies biofilm spatial organization**, independent of community composition. Using a four-species synthetic community (SynCom) grown on chemically identical but nutritionally distinct 3D-printed cellulose nanofiber (CNF) hydrogels, the authors show that **diffusible sugars promote intermixed biofilms**, whereas **polymeric substrates (CMC and xylan) drive spatial segregation**, with polymer-degrading species occupying peripheral positions. Genome annotation and metabolic modeling suggest that these spatial patterns arise from localized polymer degradation, metabolite exchange, and metabolic specialization rather than species composition alone. The study highlights that **environmental resource chemistry directly shapes biofilm architecture and microbial interactions** and provides a controlled experimental platform for studying spatial ecology. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

### Detailed Notes

#### Background

- spatial organization is a defining property of multispecies biofilms
- community function depends on
  - metabolite exchange
  - competition
  - cooperation
  - EPS production
- most biofilm studies manipulate **species composition**
- comparatively few examine how **environmental resource properties** shape spatial organization
- natural environments primarily contain **heterogeneous polymeric carbon sources**, not freely diffusible sugars

---

#### Hypothesis

Carbon substrate type alone can determine emergent biofilm architecture by imposing different spatial constraints on nutrient accessibility.

#### Experimental system

Synthetic community (SynCom)

- *Paenibacillus amylolyticus*
- *Microbacterium oxydans*
- *Stenotrophomonas rhizophila*
- *Stenotrophomonas maltophilia*

Platform

- extrusion-based 3D printing
- cellulose nanofiber (CNF) hydrogels
- identical physical scaffold
- carbon source varied independently of scaffold properties

Carbon substrates

Diffusible

- glucose
- xylose
- cellobiose

Polymeric

- CMC
- xylan

#### Major findings

##### Synergistic biofilm formation

- four-species SynCom consistently produced more biofilm biomass than any monoculture or subset
- synergy maintained across all tested carbon sources
- indicates emergent community properties rather than additive growth

---

##### Carbon substrate determines spatial organization

Diffusible sugars

- highly intermixed communities
- relatively homogeneous species distributions

Polymeric substrates

- highly structured biofilms
- spatial segregation
- localized dominant populations
- thicker biofilms
- increased aggregation

---

##### Polymer degraders become spatial organizers

On CMC and xylan

- *P. amylolyticus* localized to biofilm periphery
- surrounded or overlaid neighboring species
- likely generated localized degradation products
- created metabolic niches supporting other community members

#### CAZyme analysis

Genome annotation showed large differences in carbohydrate degradation capacity.

*P. amylolyticus*

- possessed ~3× more CAZyme genes
- encoded enzymes for
  - cellulose degradation
  - xylan degradation
  - β-glucosidase
  - endoglucanase
  - exoglucanase
  - xylanase

Other species

- limited polymer degradation capacity
- likely relied on degradation products released by *P. amylolyticus*

#### Spatial ecology

Biofilm organization emerged from interaction between

- metabolic capacity
- substrate chemistry
- diffusion limitations
- environmental context

Species interactions cannot be inferred solely from genomic metabolic potential.

---

#### Experimental innovation

Rather than printing bacteria into predefined positions,

the authors

- standardized physical environment
- varied only carbon chemistry
- allowed biofilms to self-organize naturally

This isolates the ecological effects of resource chemistry on spatial organization.

#### Limitations

- simplified four-species community
- artificial hydrogel substrate
- metabolic interactions inferred computationally rather than measured directly
- spatial metabolite exchange not experimentally quantified

Future work proposed

- spatial metabolic modeling
- stable isotope probing
