###### Abstract
The scale of opportunity to implement forest restoration exceeds billions of ha worldwide, and a part of this area in northern temperate regions includes formerly oak (Quercus spp.)-dominated ecosystems. The cost of achieving restoration is often high, and tends to increase with severity of ecosystem degradation. Therefore, it is important to develop cost-efficient regeneration practices to support forest restoration. Direct seeding of acorns, one of the earliest artificial forest regeneration techniques developed, offers several benefits including costs that can be about a third of planting oak seedlings. However, direct seeding is presently a more uncertain practice for establishing oak forests and woodlands than is planting seedlings. Much of this uncertainty can be a result of acorn depredation by rodents even though other factors may also limit oak establishment. This review provides a synthesis of foundational knowledge, particularly from Europe and North America, pertaining to the biology and ecology of acorn production, acorn germination, early seedling growth, and acorn depredation by granivorous rodents. We build on this knowledge to review research on operational direct seeding practices and four basic strategies for acorn depredation control (chemical, ecological, physical and silvicultural). We suggest that environmentally sound approaches to minimize acorn depredation and increase seedling establishment currently exists. For example, seeding operations probably hold higher chances for success if scheduled during years of high masting, if applied on open sites (areas with little shrub and tree cover), and if acorn lots are sized and sown at an appropriate depth. Several gaps in our knowledge limit the development of improved operational practices for broader success. We lack information on basic acorn biology and proper handling and storage practices for many of the oaks around the world. And, we need research to support development of new techniques or combinations of strategies and techniques that minimize acorn depredation under a wide range of site conditions. Nevertheless, our synthesis suggests that direct seeding will remain a cost-effective and environmentally sound practice for oak forest and woodland establishment, and it will likely gain expanded use as future research informs innovation that increases seed availability, decreases acorn depredation, and raises predictability of oak seedling establishment.

## seed dormancy 
- Germination is **hypogeal** across all oaks: cotyledons stay in the pericarp; radicle (root) then epicotyl (shoot) emerge sequentially.
- Dormancy is generally slight but variable — **red oaks show the greatest dormancy**, needing weeks of moist cold stratification.
- White oaks: radicle extension can begin almost immediately after shedding; but **epicotyl dormancy** still delays full germination in several species (radicle and epicotyl have different stratification requirements).
- Dormancy strength correlates positively with **latitude of origin** in several species — a genetically structured trait, not just environmental response.
- **Regional germination timing patterns:**
    - Eastern N. America/N. Europe white oaks: germinate fall of seed-drop, but epicotyl dormancy delays shoot emergence to spring
    - **Most red oaks: germinate in spring following autumn seed drop**
    - Mediterranean groups (Cerris/Ilex): flexible, autumn–late winter depending on conditions

**Viability requirements:** avoid desiccation, extreme temperature, hypoxia. Seed moisture at shedding: 30–55%; oxygen generally not limiting except in waterlogged/flooded seedbeds.

## initial seedling growth 
#### Root-first growth pattern

- Universal pattern: **large taproot develops first, then shoot** follows.
- Root growth accelerates with soil temperature; optimal ~25°C (higher than most other tree species).
- Under ideal conditions, taproot can penetrate **>100 cm in the first growing season**.
- Compacted, dry, waterlogged, or shallow soils restrict/obstruct root growth.
- First-year root growth is fueled by cotyledon reserves — these deplete faster under unfavorable conditions.
- After year one, the taproot shifts function to mostly **storage**; water/nutrient uptake shifts to lateral and fine roots.
- **Practical note relevant to nursery practice:** root-cutting (standard in bare-root seedling production) may have long-term negative consequences for drought avoidance — natural taproot development may be advantageous. This is a good talking point contrasting _direct-seeded_ vs. _planted_ stock.
#### Shoot development & flushing

- Shoot (epicotyl) growth begins after partial taproot establishment; timing varies by species/environment/epicotyl dormancy.
- Initial shoot growth can be **determinate** (terminal bud forms, growth stops for the season) under limiting light/water/nutrients.
- Under favorable conditions: **episodic shoot flushing** — multiple growth flushes per season, each preceded by a quiescent bud-forming period.
- Species-specific trade-off patterns: _Q. alba_, _Q. marilandica_, _Q. pubescens_ show root growth minimized during shoot flush periods; but this pattern was **not found in _Q. robur_** — so it's not universal across the genus.
- **Practical/silvicultural value:** presence/absence of multiple shoot flushes is a visible field indicator managers can use to judge whether competing vegetation control is needed.
#### Acorn size effects

- Positive relationship (though not universal) between **acorn mass and seedling growth/survival** across many oak species.
- **Direct Q. rubra data point:** Aizen & Woodcock (1996) found large _Q. rubra_ acorns produced seedlings that survived spring frosts better than seedlings from small acorns — good citation for your seedling-stage reading list, and directly on red oak.
- Two proposed mechanisms: (1) larger reserve pools buffer environmental stress; (2) larger acorns → larger seedlings → greater competitive ability regardless of reserve buffering.
- Practical implication: **sizing/grading acorn lots** before sowing can improve establishment success.
#### Light availability

- Oaks are only **intermediate shade-tolerant** — consistent with what you already have on Q. rubra specifically.
- _Q. robur_ seedlings can survive on as little as **2% full light** in the first growing season, but need more light in subsequent years to persist.
- **Direct Q. rubra citation:** Crow (1992) found a **5-year "half-life"** for a _Q. rubra_ seedling cohort in understory of a mixed broadleaf/conifer forest (Wisconsin) — a nice quantitative mortality figure for a lifecycle slide.
- Interesting nuance: initial carbon gain is often **not** strongly light-limited during the cotyledon-reserve phase — instead, seedlings show **plasticity in shoot:root allocation**: low light favors stem/leaf growth; high light favors root growth. Light becomes limiting mainly **after** cotyledon reserves are exhausted.
#### Competition

- Belowground competition (especially for **soil water**) is a documented driver of reduced seedling growth.
- **Direct Q. rubra citation:** Kolb & Steiner (1990) — reduced _Q. rubra_ growth when competing with grass (_Poa pratensis_), attributed to soil water competition; root/shoot growth of _Q. rubra_ decreases with decreasing soil osmotic potential (Larson & Whitmore, 1970).
- Competition effects intensify **after** cotyledon reserves deplete.
- Nuance for Mediterranean systems: shrubs can sometimes **facilitate** oak seedling establishment via shading that reduces water stress — competition effects are context-dependent, not universally negative.
#### Nutrients

- No strong evidence that soil nutrient availability limits the _initial_ germinant stage — cotyledons are thought to supply sufficient mineral nutrition through the first shoot flush.

#### Resilience mechanisms

- Young oak seedlings have several traits that buffer against stress/disturbance (frost, herbivory, fire):
    - Periodic shoot flushing (allows recovery/adjustment)
    - Sprouting ability above the root collar
    - In some species, sprouting from lignotubers/rhizomes
    - Sprouting doesn't require full seedling establishment — can occur even at early epicotyl stage if cotyledons remain functional
- **Direct Q. rubra citation:** McGraw et al. (1990) — defoliated first-year _Q. rubra_ seedlings showed **higher mortality in shaded environments** than in full sun — light availability modulates disturbance tolerance, not just baseline growth.
- Sprouting also depends on survival of belowground tissue — root hypoxia-induced mortality prevents sprouting entirely.

# PREDATION 

- Acorns are a preferred food source for insects, birds, and mammals (rodents, suids, cervids) due to high energy/nutrient content.
- Scatter-hoarding rodents (e.g., _Apodemus flavicollis_, _Peromyscus leucopus_) can act as **both predator and disperser** — cached acorns sometimes end up in favorable microsites for germination (a dual role also noted in your Q. rubra flora account for European jays/mice).
- Rodents can remove sown acorns from restoration sites **within weeks**, even when buried (Fig. 3 in paper: near-complete removal by 40–160 days depending on site type).
- Rodent population cycles are tightly linked to masting cycles — populations peak the summer after a heavy mast year due to superabundant food, and decline into early spring.
- Foraging behavior is risk-sensitive: rodents prefer low-predation-risk patches (under shelterwoods, near shrubs, slash piles, stone walls) and reduce foraging under bright moonlight or when predator scents are detected.
