###### Abstract
Pathogens that are harmless in one environment can cause a serious disease in another. Among host-associated bacteria, transition between hosts can have serious consequences for animal and human health. However, much remains unknown about how adaptation shapes bacterial distribution in the wild. Here, investigating the ecological genomics of Escherichia coli from diverse hosts and environments, we address the idea that bacteria disperse freely, and challenge the “everything is everywhere” paradigm. Using comparative genomics and parallelised high throughout pangenome-wide association studies (900 experiments) we investigate lineage distribution and identify adaptive genomic signatures associated with host species, physiology and ecology. Our findings provide insights into bacterial niche adaptation, emphasize the impact of agriculture on microbial evolution, and inform One Health frameworks by linking genomics, host ecology, and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance.

### Summary

- investigated ecological adaptation across 5,259 Escherichia coli genomes from diverse hosts
- tested the "everything is everywhere" hypothesis using comparative genomics and pangenome GWAS
- E. coli is broadly distributed across hosts but exhibits clear genomic signatures of niche adaptation
- adaptation occurs at multiple ecological scales:
  - host species
  - host physiology
  - host ecology (wild vs livestock)
- agricultural environments impose strong selective pressures, particularly for antimicrobial resistance
- supports a One Health framework linking ecology, bacterial evolution, and pathogen emergence

### Detailed Notes

#### Dataset

- 5,259 E. coli genomes
- isolates from gastrointestinal tracts of multiple vertebrate hosts
- excluded food and environmental isolates
- analyzed:
  - core genome
  - accessory genome
  - pangenome
- pangenome-wide association studies (GWAS) performed using unitig k-mers

#### Central hypothesis

##### Classical hypothesis

"Everything is everywhere, but the environment selects."

Predictions

- unrestricted bacterial dispersal
- environment determines persistence

---

##### Findings

- E. coli disperses broadly across hosts
- populations are **not** ecologically equivalent
- repeated adaptation generates host-associated genomic signatures
- ecology influences long-term lineage evolution

#### Levels of adaptation

##### Host species

Examples

- cattle
- pigs
- chickens
- deer
- wild birds

Different host species enriched for distinct accessory genes.

---

##### Host physiology

Different gut environments selected for different genomes.

Examples

- ruminants
- monogastric mammals
- birds

Selection likely driven by

- nutrient availability
- gut structure
- microbiome composition
- immune environment

---

##### Host ecology

Strong separation between

- livestock
- wild animals

Agricultural environments consistently selected for

- antimicrobial resistance
- distinct accessory genome content

---

#### Pangenome evolution

Accessory genome carried most adaptive variation.

Adaptive genes included

- metabolism
- nutrient acquisition
- stress tolerance
- antimicrobial resistance
- host colonization

Core genome remained relatively conserved.

---

#### Agriculture as an evolutionary force

Livestock environments promote

- dense host populations
- frequent transmission
- antimicrobial exposure
- increased horizontal gene transfer
- selection for resistant lineages

Agriculture shapes bacterial evolution over ecological timescales.

---

#### One Health implications

Host

↓

microbiome

↓

environment

↓

human exposure

Evolution within livestock reservoirs influences

- zoonotic emergence

- pathogen evolution

---

#### Major conclusions

- E. coli is globally distributed but locally adapted
- ecological selection produces predictable genomic signatures
- accessory genomes drive adaptation
- agriculture is a major evolutionary force shaping bacterial populations
