- ### Outline

* **Central idea**

  * I adapt by actively forming connections with people.
  * New environments can initially feel isolating.
  * Instead of withdrawing, I use curiosity, language, art, and shared interests to participate more fully.
  * Adaptability is not simply becoming comfortable; it is learning how to engage respectfully while remaining open to change.

* **Primary example: Kenya**

  * Conducted ethnobotanical fieldwork with an entirely Kenyan research team.
  * Unlike many peers, I did not have English-speaking teammates.
  * Simultaneously had to:

    * Learn unfamiliar field methods.
    * Navigate a significant language barrier.
    * Adjust to being socially isolated from my team’s conversations and jokes.
  * Initially experienced the situation as a disadvantage.
  * Reframed it as an opportunity to learn Swahili and become more engaged.
  * Used basic Swahili to:

    * Follow conversations within my research team.
    * Better understand interview participants.
    * Connect with camp staff, including the cooks.
    * Understand humor, slang, and Kenyan popular music.
  * Never became fluent, but learned to communicate despite discomfort and imperfect understanding.
  * Greater effort produced stronger relationships and made me a more attentive field researcher.
  * Lesson: adaptation requires humility, persistence, and willingness to communicate imperfectly.

* **Secondary example: Auckland**

  * Adjusted to:

    * Major time difference from home.
    * Demanding academic schedule.
    * Unfamiliar city.
    * Rapid transitions between host families.
    * Emotionally challenging coursework on colonization and Māori activism.
  * Coursework forced me to examine my own position as a student studying Māori activism at an institution built on Māori land.
  * Responded to isolation by deliberately entering unfamiliar social spaces:

    * Local knitting group.
    * Museum and bar art sessions.
    * Small punk and underground music shows.
    * Conversations with activists encountered in Auckland’s parks.
  * These relationships helped contextualize course material beyond the classroom.
  * Learned to approach difficult academic questions through listening and relationships rather than seeking immediate certainty.

* **Art and music as tools for adaptation**

  * Music helps me hear language, slang, political perspectives, and cultural influences.
  * Fiber arts create connections across differences in age, language, and background.
  * Lifelong experience with knitting, weaving, and spinning gave me an accessible way to share something personal.
  * In New Zealand, this led to connections with:

    * Flax tenders at an arboretum.
    * Rangatira teaching at a Māori arts academy.
    * Local sheep farmers.
  * Shared creative practices can open conversations that formal academic settings cannot.

* **Adapting academically**

  * Working abroad feels like speaking the same academic language with a different accent.
  * Scientific concepts may be shared, but institutions and researchers can approach questions differently.
  * Rather than treating unfamiliar methods as obstacles, I compare them with my previous training.
  * These differences expand how I formulate questions and interpret evidence.

* **Application to France/Grenoble**

  * Use French even when my language skills are imperfect.
  * Build relationships within and beyond the English-language master’s program.
  * Participate in local artistic, musical, and fiber-arts communities.
  * Approach differences in laboratory culture and scientific training with curiosity.
  * Seek help, ask questions, and remain comfortable with temporarily not understanding everything.
  * Bring the same willingness to listen and connect that helped me thrive in Kenya and Auckland.

* **Closing idea**

  * My experiences have not taught me that adjustment is easy.
  * They have taught me that isolation decreases when I actively create points of connection.
  * In Grenoble, I will adapt by remaining curious, communicating despite mistakes, and treating unfamiliarity as an invitation to learn.
