Prepare an executive summary detailing the what, where, and why of your proposed project․ If you are proposing the pursuit of a graduate degree program, summarize the program and its relevance to your career/education plans․ *****

I propose to complete the two-year Master's in Biology at Université Grenoble Alpes, beginning with the M1 in Molecular and Cellular Biology and continuing into the M2 in Microbiology, Infectious Diseases and Immunology. My undergraduate research examines how bacteriophages—viruses that infect bacteria—shape the growth and spatial organization of bacterial biofilms. At Grenoble, I will extend this work by developing the molecular, cellular, biochemical, and immunological skills necessary for translational research on persistent and antibiotic-resistant infections.

The program also combines coursework with an M1 research project, a two-month internship, and a six-month M2 internship. Within Grenoble’s network of university laboratories, national research institutes, biotechnology companies, and the university hospital, I hope to pursue internships in both academic research and industry. These experiences will expose me to complementary approaches to infectious-disease research and strengthen my independence in experimental design, data analysis, and scientific communication.

Outside the laboratory, I will continue mentoring and science outreach while engaging with Grenoble’s arts, language, and athletic communities. After the master’s, I plan to pursue an MD-PhD focused on microbial interactions and infectious disease. Ultimately, I hope to connect fundamental research on phages and biofilms with clinically useful approaches to antibiotic-resistant infection.

## Flexibility & Adaptability 1872 / 2000
Please explain how you will adapt to working and living in a new environment in the host country.

- Consider the ways in which you have grown throughout your life that make you the individual you are today.
- How have you demonstrated these qualities in your academic, professional, or personal life?
- Use specific examples from your personal experiences

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">2000 characters</mark>

I have learned to adapt to unfamiliar environments through discipline, resourcefulness, patience, and curiosity. As a high school rower, 4:45 a.m. wake-ups and ninety-minute practices taught me to build routines that sustain me under pressure. That discipline later carried me through 5:00 a.m. departures for field research in the Kenyan desert and helped me balance rigorous coursework with exploration of local beaches and mountains during a semester at the University of Auckland. Even relaxing pursuits, like teaching myself guitar, require discipline and a commitment to persist despite slow improvement.

Experience has also taught me that routines must leave room for improvisation. In Kenya, I drew on my sewing skills to repair a torn tent during a particularly windy night. As an Undergraduate Advisor, I regularly respond to unforeseen interpersonal and logistical challenges, remaining calm, assessing what students need, and connecting them with appropriate resources. These experiences strengthened my ability to use familiar skills to solve unfamiliar problems.

I have found that adapting sometimes also requires slowing down. When learning a new song, I start at 50% speed, accepting mistakes as part of my progress. I take the same approach to new cultures: I ask people to repeat unfamiliar words, observe before making assumptions, and give relationships time to develop. In Auckland, I visited the tourist sites, but I also found bird-watching spots, a favorite graffiti mural, and shopkeepers whose morning greetings I anticipated each day. Slowing down helped me contextualize my experiences and make the important transition from visitor to community member.

In Grenoble, discipline will help me meet the demands of my program, while improvisation, patience, and curiosity will allow me to create a meaningful life within unfamiliar surroundings.

I propose to complete the two-year Master in Biology at Université Grenoble Alpes, beginning with the M1 in Molecular and Cellular Biology and continuing into the M2 in Microbiology, Infectious Diseases and Immunology. Courses including Bacteriology, Infectious Diseases, and Immunobiology will strengthen my foundation in bacterial physiology and host response. The M1 research project and two-month internship will develop my independence in experimental design and analysis. In M2, Fundamental and Medical Microbiology, Pathology and Immunology, and Infectious Diseases will help me connect microbial mechanisms to disease, while biostatistics or high-throughput biology would strengthen my quantitative skills. A six-month M2 internship will integrate these perspectives.

Bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, are major forces in microbial ecology and promising tools against antibiotic-resistant infections. In the Nadell Lab at Dartmouth, I study how phages shape the growth, competition, and spatial organization of Vibrio cholerae biofilms. Biofilms structure microbial life across natural and clinical environments, where they can contribute to persistent infections. This work has taught me to examine phage-bacteria interactions at ecological and population levels. At Grenoble, I seek the molecular, biochemical, and immunological training needed to investigate how these interactions produce disease-relevant outcomes and inform new therapies.

During my internships, I hope to investigate how bacterial cell envelopes, defense mechanisms, and community organization shape phage infection, and how these processes influence persistence and therapeutic potential. I would be particularly interested in an academic internship with Cécile Breyton’s Structure and Stability of Integral Membrane Protein and Phage Assemblies Team at the Institute of Structural Biology. Its work on the initial stages of T5 infection in Escherichia coli, including host recognition and cell-wall penetration, would complement my experience with biofilm experiments and fluorescence microscopy. Structural and biochemical approaches would allow me to connect molecular events during infection with the population-level outcomes I currently study.

Grenoble’s wider research community offers complementary directions. Ina Attree’s Bacterial Pathogenesis and Cellular Responses Group studies virulence and how pathogens resist antibiotics and immune defenses. Cécile Morlot’s Pneumococcus Group examines the bacterial cell envelope and structure-guided development of antibacterials. I am also interested in an industry internship within Grenoble’s biotechnology and infectious-disease sector, where I could learn how discoveries become diagnostic or therapeutic applications. The program’s two internships would let me explore both fundamental and translational research.

Grenoble is uniquely suited to this progression. UGA draws instructors from the Institute for Advanced Biosciences, Institute of Structural Biology, and Translational Research and Innovation in Medicine and Complexity laboratory, with connections to Grenoble Alpes University Hospital. EMBL Grenoble adds expertise in structural molecular biology and infection biology. Together, the university, research institutes, hospital, and biotechnology sector create a continuum from molecular discovery to clinical and industrial application.

My preparation combines laboratory research, interdisciplinary study, and international fieldwork. As a Biology and Anthropology double major, I approach health from molecular, ecological, and human perspectives. In the Nadell Lab, I have cultured bacteria, conducted independent biofilm experiments, used fluorescence microscopy, analyzed quantitative data, and helped expand a single-species phage-biofilm system into a multispecies community for my senior thesis. In Kenya, I conducted ethnobotanical fieldwork with a Kenyan team, completed more than sixty interviews, and helped document over 120 plants used by Daasanach agropastoralists. This work taught me to collaborate across disciplinary, linguistic, and cultural differences.

France is personally as well as academically meaningful to me. My mother studied there as a young adult, and her experience first showed me how education abroad can reshape a career and sense of belonging. I grew up speaking French with family members and visiting France, giving me affection for the country while leaving me eager to understand it beyond a visitor’s perspective. Although the master’s is taught in English, my French is sufficient for daily life and community participation, and I will continue developing scientific French. Grenoble especially appeals to me because it combines the resources of a city with access to the Alps, fitting both my professional goals and the outdoor activities through which I form community.

Beyond the university, I hope to volunteer with Afev through sustained educational mentorship and participate in public arts-science programming. Language tandems, creative groups, taekwondo, and hiking will provide spaces for reciprocal exchange. My work as a teaching assistant, writing tutor, and Undergraduate Advisor has taught me that shared learning transmits curiosity, humility, and openness.

After the master’s, I plan to pursue an MD-PhD focused on microbial interactions, infectious disease, and antibiotic resistance. I hope eventually to conduct translational research connecting fundamental work on phages and biofilms with clinical questions about persistent infections. Grenoble will prepare me to move between microbial ecology, molecular mechanisms, and patient-centered application while showing me how another scientific and healthcare system approaches emerging treatments. Through Fulbright, I will gain the training, relationships, and perspective needed to build collaborations across disciplines, institutions, and countries.
