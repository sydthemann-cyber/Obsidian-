I propose to complete the two-year Master’s in Biology at Université Grenoble Alpes, beginning with the M1 in Molecular and Cellular Biology and continuing into the M2 in Microbiology, Infectious Diseases and Immunology. Courses including Bacteriology, Infectious Diseases, Fundamental and Medical Microbiology, and Pathology and Immunology will provide training across molecular, cellular, biochemical, and immunological approaches. At Grenoble, I will extend this work by studying microbial interactions across scales and developing skills in translational research. After the master’s, I plan to pursue an MD-PhD focused on microbial interactions and infectious disease. Outside the laboratory, I will continue mentoring through educational volunteering and participate in public science and arts-science programming. I will also join language exchanges and creative, athletic, and outdoor communities through writing, fiber arts, taekwondo, and hiking.


## Flexibility & Adaptability 1992 / 2000
Please explain how you will adapt to working and living in a new environment in the host country.

- Consider the ways in which you have grown throughout your life that make you the individual you are today.
- How have you demonstrated these qualities in your academic, professional, or personal life?
- Use specific examples from your personal experiences

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">2000 characters</mark>

I have learned to adapt to unfamiliar environments through discipline, resourcefulness, patience, and curiosity. As a high school rower, 4:45 a.m. wake-ups and ninety-minute practices taught me to build routines under pressure. That discipline later carried me through 5:00 a.m. departures for field research in the Kenyan desert and helped me balance coursework with exploration during a semester at the University of Auckland. Even teaching myself guitar requires discipline and a commitment to persist despite slow improvement.

Experience has also taught me that routines must leave room for improvisation. In Kenya, I drew on my sewing skills to repair a torn tent during a windy night. As an Undergraduate Advisor, I solve unexpected interpersonal and logistical problems by staying flexible and identifying available resources. Research requires the same willingness to pivot. When overlapping fluorescence complicated my image analysis, I drew on my experience with conjugation to create a new fluorescent bacterial construct. These experiences strengthened my ability to use familiar skills to solve unfamiliar problems.

I have found that adapting sometimes also requires slowing down. When learning a new song, I start at 50% speed, accepting mistakes as part of my progress. I take the same approach to new cultures: I ask people to repeat unfamiliar words, observe before making assumptions, and accept that new places and relationships may feel uncomfortable before they feel familiar. In Auckland, I visited the tourist sites, but I also found bird-watching spots, a favorite graffiti mural, and shopkeepers whose morning greetings I anticipated each day. Slowing down helped me contextualize my experiences and transition from visitor to community member.

In Grenoble, discipline will help me meet the demands of my program, while improvisation, patience, and curiosity will help me respond to unexpected challenges and build a meaningful life within unfamiliar surroundings.
