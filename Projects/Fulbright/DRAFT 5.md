## Abstract/Summary of Proposal  1990 / 1750 

**Prepare an executive summary detailing the what, where, and why of your proposed project․ If you are proposing the pursuit of a graduate degree program, summarize the program and its relevance to your career/education plans․ ***

**Links to websites are not permitted.**

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">1750 characters</mark>

I propose to complete the two-year Master’s in Biology at Université Grenoble Alpes to prepare for an MD-PhD investigating antibiotic resistance. Beginning with the M1 in Molecular and Cellular Biology and continuing into the M2 in Microbiology, Infectious Diseases and Immunology, the program integrates coursework with an M1 research project, a two-month internship, and a six-month M2 internship.

I will build on my current research in microbial interactions by investigating related questions across biological scales—from molecular mechanisms to microbial communities and host responses—and through Grenoble’s diverse structural, biochemical, imaging, and clinical approaches. This broader perspective will help me understand how microbial processes shape infection and treatment. Courses including Fundamental and Medical Microbiology and Pathology and Immunology will provide the interdisciplinary foundation necessary to connect microbial mechanisms with clinical outcomes. Grenoble’s ecosystem of national research institutes, biotechnology companies, and university hospitals will expose me to the full research pipeline, from fundamental discovery to clinical translation, while the program’s internships will provide experience across academic, industry, and healthcare settings—a breadth of training central to my planned MD-PhD trajectory.

My development in Grenoble will extend beyond formal coursework and research. Through educational volunteering and public science programming I will continue the mentoring work I began at Dartmouth while growing as an educator and scientific communicator. Participation in Grenoble’s creative, athletic, and outdoor communities will allow me to share my interests while building relationships through cultural exchange. Together, these experiences will strengthen the communication, mentorship, and cross-cultural collaboration skills essential to my goal of connecting fundamental microbiological research with patient care as an MD-PhD.

**Please explain how you will adapt to working and living in a new environment in the host country.**

- **Consider the ways in which you have grown throughout your life that make you the individual you are today.**
    
- **How have you demonstrated these qualities in your academic, professional, or personal life?**
    
- **Use specific examples from your personal experiences**
    

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">2000 characters</mark>

Junior year of high-school I left my native D.C. to live and learn on a farm in the Green Mountains of Vermont. I knew nothing of harsh New England winters or farming, but I would soon learn. My time in Vermont was filled with new experiences–assisting a lamb-birth was one of the more notable–and they taught me a valuable lesson: the most impactful experiences often begin with a leap of faith into the unknown.

After my freshman year I embarked on a 7 week research expedition to Kenya. Armed with a tent, a duffel bag, and a field guide, I experienced violent wind storms and searing desert temperatures. In my travels across the uninhabited highlands of Iceland on horseback I encountered snowstorms, volcanic terrain, and melting glaciers. 

But my lack of familiarity did not mean I was unprepared. Dartmouth’s Polar Bear plunge, where you jump into a frozen pond–may have been unfamiliar, but the campus ethos of wilderness exploration had already been instilled in me from my time in Vermont. In Kenya I used my sewing skills to repair holes in my tent and drew on my knack for languages to overcome barriers between me and my Swahili-speaking research team. When my mother fell off her horse in Iceland and broke her ankle, I used a ripped shirt and riding crop to splint her leg.  

Each time I set off on a new adventure, I become more comfortable with uncertainty and more capable of applying lessons from previous experiences. In Auckland, I was initially nervous about living with a host family, but international travel had taught me to seek connection through small points of commonality. We soon bonded over science fiction and music. This summer, while assisting a course on Research and Lab Work in Medicine I faced the unfamiliar tasks of directing laboratory sessions and delivering lectures. My research experience allowed me to guide students confidently through their experiments, while presentations in unconventional settings—like the Kenyan desert—had prepared me to speak with composure. Adaptability is a quality I have deliberately cultivated by repeatedly entering the unknown and carrying each lesson into the next challenge.

More broadly, UGA would allow me to investigate a central research interest—how phages interact with bacterial communities—from ecological, molecular, structural, and immunological perspectives.

Grenoble’s wider research community offers many complementary opportunities. Ina Attree’s Bacterial Pathogenesis and Cellular Responses Group studies virulence and immune defenses, while Cécile Morlot’s Pneumococcus Group examines the development of antibacterials - both very relevant fields of study for me. I am also interested in an industry internship within Grenoble’s biotechnology or infectious-disease sector, such as bioMérieux’s internship program, where I could learn how basic science discoveries are developed into diagnostic or therapeutic applications. Together, the two internships would expose me to multiple stages of the fundamental-to-translational research pipeline.

Grenoble is uniquely suited to this progression. UGA draws instructors from the Institute for Advanced Biosciences, Institute of Structural Biology, and Translational Research and Innovation in Medicine and Complexity laboratory, with connections to Grenoble Alpes University Hospital. Together, the university, research institutes, hospital, and biotechnology sector create a continuum from molecular discovery to clinical and industrial application.

Coursework in cell biology, microbiology, biochemistry, genetics, and ecology has prepared me to study biological processes across molecular and population scales. As a Biology and Anthropology double major, I approach health through molecular, ecological, and human perspectives. During fieldwork in Kenya, I conducted research within an interdisciplinary Kenyan team despite linguistic and cultural barriers. I also hope to participate in extracurricular clubs, taekwondo, and hiking communities.
