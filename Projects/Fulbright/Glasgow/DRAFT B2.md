## **Flexibility & Adaptability 1951 / 2000** 

Please explain how you will adapt to working and living in a new environment in the host country.

* Consider the ways in which you have grown throughout your life that make you the individual you are today.  
* How have you demonstrated these qualities in your academic, professional, or personal life?  
* Use specific examples from your personal experiences

2000 characters  
Junior year of high-school I left my native Washington DC to live and learn on a farm in the Green Mountains of Vermont, despite knowing nothing of harsh New England winters or farming. My time in Vermont was filled with new experiences–assisting a lamb-birth was one of the more notable–and taught me a valuable lesson: the most impactful experiences often begin with a leap of faith into the unknown.  
After my freshman year of college I embarked on a 7 week research expedition to the Turkana Basin in Kenya. \[f\]Armed with a tent and duffel bag, I experienced violent wind storms and searing desert temperatures. Travels later that year across the uninhabited highlands of Iceland on horseback brought close encounters with snowstorms, volcanic terrain, and melting glaciers. But a lack of familiarity did not mean I was unprepared. In Kenya I used my sewing skills to repair holes in my tent and drew on my knack for languages to bond with my Swahili-speaking research team. When my mother fell off her horse in Iceland, I used a ripped shirt and riding crop to splint her broken ankle.    
Each time I set off on a new adventure, I become more comfortable with uncertainty and more capable of drawing on past experiences. For my semester in New Zealand, I was initially nervous about living with a host family, but international travel had taught me to seek connection through small points of commonality. We soon bonded over science fiction and music. This summer, while assisting a course on Research and Lab Work in Medicine I faced the unfamiliar tasks of directing laboratory sessions and delivering lectures. My research experience helped me guide students confidently through their experiments, while presentations in unconventional settings—like the Kenyan desert—had prepared me to speak with composure. Adaptability is a quality I have deliberately cultivated by repeatedly entering the unknown and carrying each lesson into the next challenge.\[g\]

The confidence I have developed as a student, researcher, and athlete reflects the guidance of mentors who welcomed me into unfamiliar communities. I strive to offer others that same support. As co-captain of the Taekwondo Club and co-founder of the Creative Writing Club at Dartmouth, I help students develop new skills and find fellowship. As a writing tutor, teaching assistant, and Undergraduate Advisor, I encourage my peers to grow more confident in classrooms, laboratories, and residence halls. Watching them develop expertise and enthusiasm has been the most rewarding aspect of these roles.  
In Grenoble, I hope to continue tutoring and academic advising through organizations like Afev and La Casemate, supporting students’ growth and proficiency in the sciences.\[h\] I also hope to extend my efforts to connect the arts and sciences. At Dartmouth, I recruited STEM students to the Creative Writing Club, where techniques such as building a compelling narrative strengthen both creative and scientific communication. Through La Casemate’s public-science programming and Atelier Arts Sciences’ collaborations between artists and researchers, I look forward to exploring new ways to make science engaging, creative, and accessible\[i\].

In Kenya, I began a friendship-bracelet circle with local peers, exchanging patterns while discussing our families and hometowns. Because everyone participated as both teacher and learner, a simple activity became a natural form of cultural exchange. In Auckland I enjoyed making traditional apple pie for my host family, and they reciprocated by introducing me to every variety of New Zealand meat pie on offer (I loved them all\!).\[j\]  
Through mentorship, shared learning, and curiosity, I hope to bring an American optimism for growth and innovation, while developing a nuanced understanding of French education, science, and everyday life.

I propose to complete the one-year MSc in Infection Biology at the University of Glasgow, following the Microbiology (Bacteriology) track. I am currently double-majoring in Biology and Anthropology at Dartmouth College. By studying biological processes across scales—from interactions within microbial communities to evolutionary change across populations—I have learned to approach questions in each field from new perspectives. For example, microbiology helps me understand the mechanisms through which infections develop, while anthropology encourages me to consider how clinical practices, social conditions, and public-health systems shape their effects on human communities. My current research in the Nadell Lab focuses on interactions between bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, and biofilms, spatially structured communities of microorganisms. At Glasgow, I plan to continue investigating these interactions in preparation for an MD-PhD focused on translating fundamental microbiological research into new treatments for antibiotic-resistant infections.

Glasgow’s courses cover foundational topics in infection biology, including host–pathogen interactions and animal models. I am particularly drawn, however, to the program’s emphasis on developing independent researchers through courses such as “Designing a Research Project” and “Research Skills for Immunology and Infection Biology.” During the program’s 14-week summer research project, I hope to work with Dr. Giusy Mariano, who combines methods including single-cell microscopy and comparative genomics to identify important phage-defense pathways. Her research directly connects my previous experience with my future goals while offering me the opportunity to learn new methods. I would also be excited to work with Dr. Liam Rooney, who uses advanced optical techniques to investigate host–pathogen interactions, or Dr. Justine Rudkin, who uses functional genomics to examine how different environments shape Staphylococcus aureus pathogenicity.

Beyond developing my research skills, Glasgow’s curriculum emphasizes the progression from fundamental discovery to clinical and commercial application. Courses such as “Current Trends in Biomedical Research” and “Technology Transfer and Commercialisation” would prepare me to move confidently between academic and industry settings upon returning to the United States. Glasgow’s closely connected research community would allow me to observe this translational process firsthand. The University collaborates with NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, Public Health Scotland, local government, and industry, bringing laboratory researchers into conversation with clinicians, public-health practitioners, and policymakers. These relationships support work spanning basic microbiology, clinical investigation, pathogen surveillance, and responses to antimicrobial resistance. Training within this network would teach me not only how discoveries are made, but also how they can be developed into clinical interventions and public-health strategies.
