## Central academic angle

Your clearest Glasgow narrative is:

- Build from studying the ecological and spatial outcomes of phage–biofilm interactions in _Vibrio cholerae_.
    
- Learn to investigate the same general problem at additional scales:
    
    - Molecular antiphage-defense mechanisms

    - Multispecies microbial interactions
        
    - Host-associated colonization and infection

This is a slightly tighter research story than the Grenoble application because Mariano, Rooney, Roe, and Rudkin all connect directly to different dimensions of your existing work.

## MSc Infection Biology information

- One-year, full-time MSc based in the School of Infection & Immunity.
    
- You would select the **Microbiology (Bacteriology) specialism** after arriving.
    
- Ninety credits, including the research project, would relate to that specialism.
    
- The degree consists of six taught courses and an independent research project or dissertation.
    
- Relevant core courses:
    
    - _Host–Pathogen Interactions and Immune Responses to Infection_
        
    - _Research Skills for Immunology and Infection Biology_
        
    - _Designing a Research Project: Infection Biology_
        
    - _MSc Bioscience Research Project_
        
- Most relevant optional courses:
    
    - _Bio-Imaging for Research Scientists_ — exceptionally strong connection to your confocal microscopy, BiofilmQ, 3D Slicer, and spatial analysis experience.
        
    - _Omic Analyses for the Biomedical Sciences: From Genomics to Metabolomics_ — would help you move from imaging and population outcomes toward genomic and molecular analyses of phage defense and microbial interactions.
        
    - _Diagnostic Technologies and Devices_ — connects microbial research with clinical detection and patient care.
        
    - _Technology Transfer and Commercialisation of Bioscience Research_ — useful for understanding how phage therapies, antimicrobials, or diagnostics move beyond academic laboratories.
        
    - _Animal Models of Disease_ — useful if you want to emphasize progression toward host-relevant and translational models.
        
- The programme specifically advertises:
    
    - Biological imaging and high-content screening microscopy
        
    - Genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics facilities
        
    - Interaction with clinical and industrial scientists
        
    - Guest lecturers from pharmaceutical, diagnostic, and biotechnology sectors
        
    - Basic, translational, and clinical research
        
- About half of its graduates reportedly enter research careers, including PhD study and clinical or academic laboratories. This fits your MD-PhD trajectory. [MSc programme](https://www.gla.ac.uk/postgraduate/taught/infectionbiologywithspecialisms/)
    

## Researchers to mention

I would not give all four equal space in the final essays. Use Mariano and Rooney as the central pairing, then Roe and Rudkin to demonstrate the surrounding intellectual community.

### Dr Giusy Mariano: primary research fit

- Studies bacterial antiphage systems and phage counter-defenses.
    
- Investigates phage–bacteria evolutionary dynamics and their consequences for phage therapy.
    
- Works with _Pseudomonas aeruginosa_, _Serratia marcescens_, and _E. coli_.
    
- Uses comparative genomics, genetics, biochemistry, single-cell microscopy, and structural biology.
    
- Explicitly seeks to study antiphage systems in physiologically relevant contexts.
    
- Best connection to your work:
    
    - You currently observe how phages alter bacterial competition and community structure.
        
    - Her laboratory could help you investigate the molecular mechanisms producing those population-level outcomes.
        
    - Both projects ask how biological context changes the result of phage predation.
        
    - This would also move you closer to evaluating the promises and limitations of phage therapy.
        

Possible application language:

- “Dr Giusy Mariano’s research on antiphage defense systems and phage–bacteria coevolution would allow me to connect the community-level outcomes I currently observe with their underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms.”
    

[Mariano profile](https://www.gla.ac.uk/schools/infectionimmunity/staff/giusymariano/)

### Dr Liam Rooney: primary methodological fit

- Studies the spatial organization of microbial infections.
    
- Develops advanced optical-imaging and image-analysis methods.
    
- Focuses on biofilm-driven disease, antimicrobial resistance, host–pathogen interactions, and image-based diagnostics.

    - Direct continuation of your experience with confocal imaging, microfluidics, and three-dimensional biofilm analysis.
        
    - Opportunity to examine how biofilm structure controls exposure or susceptibility to phages and antimicrobials.

Possible application language:

[Rooney profile](https://www.gla.ac.uk/schools/infectionimmunity/staff/liamrooney/)

Possible application language:

[Roe Research Group](https://www.gla.ac.uk/schools/infectionimmunity/staff/andrewroe/roeresearchgroup/)

### Dr Justine Rudkin: complementary multispecies and host-associated fit

## Academic culture and research environment

These are the strongest claims you can credibly make about Glasgow’s academic culture:

- **Collaboration across biological scales.** Your four researchers span molecular defense systems, imaging, community ecology, host colonization, and therapeutic development.
    
- **Basic-to-clinical continuum.** The School explicitly combines basic, translational, and clinical infection research and brings clinical and industrial scientists into the MSc.
    
- **Shared technology rather than isolated laboratories.** The programme highlights imaging, high-content microscopy, polyomics, and bioinformatics resources.
    
- **University–NHS relationships.** The Glasgow University Microbiome Initiative brings together more than 40 research groups across the university and NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, covering human health, animal health, the environment, bioinformatics, and translation. This is useful for your MD-PhD framing. [Glasgow University Microbiome Initiative](https://www.gla.ac.uk/research/az/gumi/)
    
- **Early-career researchers are intellectually visible.** Mariano, Rooney, and Rudkin are building focused research programmes, while Roe provides a larger established group. That combination could offer close mentorship alongside a broad institutional network.
    
- **Interdisciplinary compatibility.** Glasgow is a good place to retain your anthropology-informed view of health rather than presenting yourself exclusively as a bench scientist.
    

A useful contrast with Dartmouth—not stated competitively—is that Glasgow would expose you to:

- A national healthcare system
    
- Closer institutional connections between university laboratories and NHS clinicians
    
- A different structure for postgraduate scientific training
    
- British and Scottish approaches to antimicrobial resistance, research translation, and public engagement

## Why Glasgow specifically

I think your strongest city-specific angle combines **science, creativity, and social history**.

### 1. A city where science and medicine are embedded in public institutions

- The research environment connects university scientists with NHS clinicians and wider public-health systems.
    
- That matters because you want to become a physician-scientist rather than pursue isolated laboratory work.
    
- You would be learning not only new microbiological methods but also how research operates alongside a national healthcare system.
    

### 2. A large creative city that complements your scientific identity

- Glasgow is a UNESCO City of Music and contains more than 100 cultural organizations.
    
- Its writing, music, museums, and public events would allow you to continue treating science and the arts as mutually reinforcing.
    
- This is more distinctive to you than a generic claim about liking restaurants, architecture, or city life. [Glasgow’s cultural environment](https://www.gla.ac.uk/colleges/arts/aboutus/)
    

### 3. Urban life with access to Scotland’s landscapes

- Glasgow gives you the community and cultural resources of a city while the Mountaineering Club provides a practical route into the Highlands.
    
- This makes a good continuation of how you have built relationships through outdoor experiences in Vermont, Kenya, Iceland, New Zealand, and New Hampshire.
    
- The emphasis should be on how outdoor activities help you form relationships—not simply that Scotland is beautiful.
    

### 4. A place to examine intertwined Scottish and Caribbean histories

This could be meaningful, but it requires more nuance than “my ancestors were Scottish.”

- Your Jamaican grandmother’s Scottish ancestry gives you a personal reason to be curious about connections between Scotland and the Caribbean.
    
- Glasgow’s historical relationship with Jamaica was shaped heavily by commerce, empire, and slavery.
    
- The University has formally investigated its receipt of slavery-derived wealth and established a long-term reparative-justice partnership with the University of the West Indies.
    
- Because you are also an anthropology major, this could become a thoughtful secondary reason for choosing Glasgow: exploring how family histories sit within larger histories of movement, colonialism, and exchange.
    
- The University has committed £20 million over two decades to reparative-justice work with the University of the West Indies. [UofG–UWI partnership](https://www.gla.ac.uk/explore/historicalslaveryinitiative/uwi/)
    

I would phrase this cautiously:

> My Jamaican grandmother’s family history includes Scottish ancestry, giving me a personal curiosity about the long and often difficult connections between Scotland and the Caribbean. Glasgow’s efforts to confront its historical relationship with the West Indies, including its reparative-justice partnership with the University of the West Indies, would offer an important setting in which to understand that history more fully.

That is potentially compelling because it joins your family history with your anthropological interest in how individual lives intersect with institutions and historical forces.

I would **not mention that your mother was conceived in Edinburgh**. It is memorable, but it does not establish a substantive relationship with Scotland and could distract from your stronger academic and family-history reasons.
