Working title: Negative Frequency Dependence in Multispecies Marine Biofilms

This project uses laboratory experiments to expand our understanding of phage–bacteria interactions. Bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, strongly influence bacterial competition, evolution, and pathogenicity. During the lytic cycle, phages replicate within bacterial cells and cause immediate cell lysis, which kills the cell. During the lysogenic cycle, the phage genome integrates into the bacterial genome, and the resulting phage-carrying bacterium is called a lysogen. Temperate phages can alternate between these two cycles. Instead of killing bacteria in a lytic cycle, phages in a lysogenic cycle can actually provide fitness benefits to their hosts by protecting them from infection with the same or closely related phages, a process known as superinfection immunity.

In nature, bacteria frequently live in biofilms—static, spatially heterogeneous communities that are widespread in natural environments and can contribute to antibiotic-resistant infections in humans. Many marine biofilms develop on chitin, a carbohydrate polymer that is the primary component of marine snow. Previous research in the Nadell Lab used two strains of Vibrio cholerae, a bacterium that naturally colonizes chitin, and found that lysogens carrying a temperate lambda phage experience negative frequency-dependent selection when competing with phage-susceptible bacteria in biofilms. At low frequencies, lysogens benefit because their released phages kill susceptible competitors, creating space for further chitin colonization. This benefit offsets the physical (fitness) cost of producing phages. At high frequencies, however, released phages are more likely to adsorb to nearby lysogens protected by superinfection immunity, than to encounter susceptible cells. Lysogens therefore incur the cost of phage production without benefiting from the death of competitors. This results in a negative relationship between initial lysogen density and change in lysogen frequency.

Natural biofilms contain multiple bacterial species, including species resistant to a given phage. This project will test whether negative frequency-dependent selection persists in a multispecies community containing both lysogenic and susceptible strains of V. cholerae, as well as phage-resistant Aeromonas. Fluorescently labeled bacteria will be grown in a microfluidic device that mimics conditions associated with marine snow, and confocal fluorescence microscopy will generate data for quantitative image analysis. This work will expand on fundamental knowledge of phage–bacteria dynamics and may contribute to a future publication.
