This project uses laboratory experiments to examine how bacterial community composition affects bacteriophage propagation through biofilms. Bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, shape bacterial communities by killing susceptible cells, providing immunity to phage-carrying cells, and moving genes both between cells and across generations (horizontal and vertical transmission). Phages are now understood to influence bacterial pathogenicity, competition, and evolution. Understanding these interactions is especially important because biofilms are widespread in nature and contribute to antibiotic-resistant infections in humans. Previous research in the Nadell Laboratory found that temperate phages, which can integrate into bacterial genomes, spread through single-species Vibrio cholerae growing on chitin, the primary component of marine snow. However, phage spread is limited by the biofilms' spatial structure and the viral particles' ability to access susceptible hosts. Natural biofilms contain multiple bacterial species that may alter both biofilm architecture and the movement of phage particles, making their effects on phage propagation difficult to predict. This project will examine these effects in a community containing phage-carrying and phage-susceptible V. cholerae together with phage-resistant Aeromonas. Fluorescently labeled bacteria will be grown on chitin in a microfluidic device, visualized using confocal fluorescence microscopy, and measured through quantitative image analysis. This work will clarify how community composition and spatial structure shape phage propagation and bacterial population dynamics in biofilms, and may contribute to a future publication.
