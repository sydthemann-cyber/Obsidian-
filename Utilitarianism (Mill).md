Chapter 2:

### The Greatest Happiness Principle 

“The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that **actions** are right **in proportion as** they **tend to promote** happiness, wrong as they **tend to** produce the **reverse of happiness**. By happiness is intended **pleasure, and the absence of pain**; by unhappiness, pain, and the privation of pleasure.”

actions: only evaluates consequences of actions, not motives
right in proportion: there are shades of morality
tend to: basic or average tendency of the act, this 'kind of act', not necessarily the specific results 
- expected value sum: (probability x value)
hedonism / the theory of the good / epicurian 
happiness: not the agents happiness, but the greatest amount of happiness total 
scope: whole sentient creation, anything that can feel pleasure or pain 

objections:
- if pleasure is only good, then human life / pleasures are no higher than a pigs
	- there are human pleasures that are higher than this, like imagination, hard work, ethics
		- “It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, is of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the question.”
		- should also consider quality and quantity (2 competent judges test)
	- --> some people still choose the lower pleasure, the desire for noble feelings can be killed in people, weakness of character (not because they are better)
	- --> what makes a pleasure higher, dignity 
- experience machine 
	- what do we value in reality? --> can change to preference satisfaction 
- too demanding
	- Most good actions are aimed at “the benefit of individuals,” not the world. Only a public benefactor needs to think about society at large.
- expedience 
	- Any lie, “even unintentional,” weakens “the trustworthiness of human assertion, which is not only the principal support of all present social well-being, but the insufficiency of which does more than any one thing… to keep back civilisation, virtue, everything on which human happiness on the largest scale depends.”
	- focus on the tendency 
- **justice**
	- different origin than utilitarianism 
		- justice is a subclass of utility 
		- “The idea of justice supposes two things: a rule of conduct, and a sentiment which sanctions the rule.”
			- breaking faith
			- partiality 
			- 'conformity to the law that ought to exist'
				- Moral obligation in general: an act is wrong if the person ought to be punished for it, whether by law, by public opinion, or by their own conscience.
			- “**Justice implies something which it is not only right to do, and wrong not to do, but which some individual person can claim from us as his moral right.** No one has a moral right to our generosity or beneficence.”
		- That gives **perfect duties**, which have a corresponding right-holder: don’t kill, repay debts. **Imperfect duties**, like charity, have no specific claimant.

- Rights: something society should defend you in the defense of 
	- “To have a right, then, is… to have something which society ought to defend me in the possession of. If the objector goes on to ask why it ought, I can give him no other reason than general utility.”
- security:
	- The chief interest a right protects is **security**, “the most vital of all interests.” Everything else depends on it, and no one can do without it. That explains the “peculiar energy” of the feeling of justice.
- perfect duty: correlative right in some other person 
- imperfect duty: something you should do, but not specific
